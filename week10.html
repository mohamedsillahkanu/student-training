When a pie is right

When the message is a share: this region holds a quarter of the national burden; two thirds of suspected cases were tested. Not when the message is a ranking or a trend.

How one is built

Summarise to a few rows, check they add to the total you mean, then map everything to fill on a single stacked bar and bend it with coord_polar().

What happensillustration

A pie chart answers one question only

What share of the whole is each part? Not which is biggest, not how they have changed, not how much: just the shares. If your sentence about the chart does not contain the words "of the total", a pie is the wrong chart.

ggplot2 has no geom_pie, and that is deliberate

You build one from a stacked bar and bend it round with coord_polar(). It feels like a trick the first time. It is a trick, and the reason it exists is that the authors of ggplot2 think you should have to mean it: people read angles badly, and a bar chart of the same numbers is easier to read almost every time.

When a pie is genuinely the right choice

Two to five parts, adding to a meaningful whole, where the message is one big share rather than a ranking. A treatment cascade, of everyone suspected, how many were tested, and of those how many were positive, is a good pie. Sixteen districts is not, and this module will show you why with your own data.

Two rules before you draw one

The parts must add to the whole, with nothing counted twice and nothing left out. And the whole must mean something: a pie of confirmed cases by district works because every case is in exactly one district. A pie of "facilities reporting" plus "cases confirmed" is nonsense, because those are not parts of the same thing.

2Check the parts add to the whole

Explanation
  • This is the check that makes a pie honest: the slices must add to the total you claim they add to.
  • If they do not, something is double counted or dropped, usually a missing value, or a category that appears in two groups.
  • Do it every time. A pie that does not sum to its whole is not a mistake in the chart, it is a mistake in the analysis.

4The stacked bar a pie is made from

ggplot(by_region, aes(x = "", y = confirmed, fill = region)) + geom_col(width = 1, colour = "white")
R Consoleoutput
# one tall bar, five colours stacked
Explanation
  • x = "" is the trick: every row shares one x position, so the bars stack into a single column.
  • width = 1 removes the gap either side, so when it is bent round there is no wedge missing.
  • This is a real chart in its own right. If you stop here you have a stacked bar, which many readers find easier than the pie.

5Bend it into a pie

ggplot(by_region, aes(x = "", y = confirmed, fill = region)) + geom_col(width = 1, colour = "white") + coord_polar(theta = "y") + theme_void()
Plots panefigure

four lines: the data, the bar, the bend, the theme

Explanation
  • coord_polar(theta = "y") wraps the y axis around a circle, so bar lengths become angles. That one line is the whole pie chart.
  • theta = "y" matters. With theta = "x" you get a bullseye of rings instead, which is a different chart entirely.
  • theme_void() removes the axes, which on a pie are meaningless, nobody needs a y axis in degrees.

When to use it

Every pie you show anybody. Without labels a reader can tell a quarter from a half and nothing finer, so the percentages have to be on the chart or in the legend.

How to use it

geom_text() with position_stack(vjust = 0.5) puts a label in the middle of each slice. fct_reorder() or a factor with levels controls the order, and scale_fill_brewer() replaces the default colours.

What happensillustration

1Percentages inside the slices

ggplot(by_region, aes(x = "", y = confirmed, fill = region)) + geom_col(width = 1, colour = "white") + geom_text(aes(label = paste0(share, "%")), position = position_stack(vjust = 0.5), colour = "white", size = 3.6) + coord_polar(theta = "y") + theme_void()
Plots panefigure

labels at the middle of each slice, in white

Explanation
  • position_stack(vjust = 0.5) is what places a label halfway along each slice. Without it every label lands at the slice boundary.
  • paste0(share, "%") builds the text from the column you computed on the last card, so the chart and the report cannot disagree.
  • White text reads on dark slices and vanishes on pale ones. Check your smallest slice before you publish.

2Labels outside, when the slices are thin

ggplot(by_region, aes(x = "", y = confirmed, fill = region)) + geom_col(width = 1, colour = "white") + geom_text(aes(label = paste0(region, "\n", share, "%")), position = position_stack(vjust = 0.5), size = 3, hjust = 0.5) + coord_polar(theta = "y") + theme_void() + theme(legend.position = "none")
Plots panefigure

names on the chart, legend switched off

Explanation
  • Naming the region on the label lets you drop the legend entirely, which gives the pie itself more of the page.
  • \n puts the percentage on a second line so a long region name does not overlap its neighbour.
  • Western is 6.3 per cent, about 23 degrees. Any slice under about 5 per cent will need its label outside or a leader line, which is the point at which a bar chart starts winning.

3Ordering the slices

by_region <- by_region %>% mutate(region = factor(region, levels = region[order(-confirmed)])) ggplot(by_region, aes(x = "", y = confirmed, fill = region)) + geom_col(width = 1, colour = "white") + coord_polar(theta = "y") + theme_void()
Plots panefigure

largest first, clockwise from twelve o'clock

Explanation
  • Slices are drawn in factor order, so setting the levels sets the order, largest first, clockwise from the top.
  • Alphabetical order is the default and it scatters the big and small slices around the circle, which makes comparing them harder than it needs to be.
  • One convention, kept consistently: largest first. A reader who has seen one of your pies then knows where to look on the next.

4Choosing the colours

ggplot(by_region, aes(x = "", y = confirmed, fill = region)) + geom_col(width = 1, colour = "white") + coord_polar(theta = "y") + scale_fill_brewer(palette = "Set2", name = "Region") + theme_void()
Plots panefigure

Set2: five distinct colours, no implied order

Explanation
  • A pie needs a qualitative palette: colours that differ without implying an order. "Set2", "Dark2" and "Paired" are the usual choices.
  • Never use a sequential palette like Blues on a pie. Shades of one colour imply a ranking that the slices themselves already show, and adjacent slices become hard to tell apart.
  • scale_fill_manual(values = c(...)) when a region must always be the same colour across a whole report.

5Pulling one slice out

by_region <- by_region %>% mutate(highlight = region == "Western") ggplot(by_region, aes(x = "", y = confirmed, fill = region)) + geom_col(width = 1, colour = "white") + coord_polar(theta = "y") + scale_fill_manual(values = c("Western" = "#cc5f3d", "North East" = "grey80", "Southern" = "grey80", "Eastern" = "grey80", "North West" = "grey80")) + theme_void()
Plots panefigure

one slice in colour, the rest as context

Explanation
  • Greying out everything except one slice is the honest version of an exploded pie: it points at one share without distorting the geometry.
  • scale_fill_manual() with a named vector gives you exact control over which category gets which colour.
  • This is the pie worth making when the message is a single number, "the Western Area accounts for only six per cent of confirmed cases": and the rest is context.

How to use it

When to use it

Before it goes into a bulletin. A pie needs one thing other charts do not: the total somewhere on the chart, because percentages of an unstated whole are unusable.

How to use it

labs() for the words with the total in the subtitle, theme(legend.position) to place the key, and ggsave() with a square shape: a circle in a wide frame wastes the page.

5The checklist before you publish one

# 1. do the parts add to the whole? sum(by_region$confirmed) == sum(cases$confirmed, na.rm = TRUE) # 2. how many slices? nrow(by_region) # 3. is the smallest slice readable? min(by_region$share)

Explanation
  • Three checks, three lines. They catch the mistakes that make a pie misleading rather than merely ugly.
  • TRUE, five slices, and nothing smaller than about 5 per cent means the chart is safe to publish. A FALSE, or nine slices, or a 0.8 per cent slice means go back.
  • Keep these three lines above every pie in your script. They cost nothing and they are the difference between a chart you can defend and one you cannot.

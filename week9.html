When to draw one

Straight after the read, on any column you are about to average, total or set a threshold on. Ten seconds of looking prevents a report built on a number that describes nobody.

How it works

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_histogram(). The raw rows go in, R cuts the column into bins and counts them, and the y axis is that count. Nothing is summarised first.

What happensillustration

A histogram answers a different question from a bar chart

A bar chart shows one number per category: six districts, six bars. A histogram takes one numeric column and shows its shape, how many facility months reported 50 to 100 suspected cases, how many reported 100 to 150, and so on. There are no categories in the data at all; the groups are cut out of the numbers by R.

You do not summarise first

This is the one chart in the course that takes the raw rows. Week 7 made you collapse 1,248 records into 16 before plotting. Here you hand ggplot() all 1,248 and it does the counting, which is why the y axis says count and you never map it yourself.

Why it matters in routine data

The mean suspected case load is 181 a month. On its own that number hides everything: whether most facilities sit near 181 or whether half see 60 and half see 300, whether there is a second cluster of very busy facilities, and whether anything impossible got into the file. The histogram shows all of that in one picture, which is why it belongs at the start of an analysis rather than the end.

1Read the file and look at the column

library(pacman) p_load(readr, dplyr, ggplot2) cases <- read_csv("routine_cases_dummy.csv") %>% distinct() summary(cases$suspected)
R Consoleoutput
   Min. 1st Qu.  Median    Mean 3rd Qu.    Max.
   47.0   126.0   177.0   180.9   233.0   377.0
Explanation
  • distinct() drops the duplicated record, leaving 1,248 facility months, one per chiefdom per month.
  • Mean 180.9 and median 177 sit close together, which hints at a roughly symmetric shape. The histogram is how you check that hint.
  • The quartiles already tell you the middle half of facilities report between 126 and 233 suspected cases. Everything else this module does is a picture of that.

2The count, the mean and the standard deviation

cases %>% summarise( n = n(), mean = round(mean(suspected), 1), median = median(suspected), sd = round(sd(suspected), 1), min = min(suspected), max = max(suspected))
R Consoleoutput
# A tibble: 1 x 6
      n  mean median    sd   min   max
  <int> <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
1  1248  180.    177  69.0    47   377
Explanation
  • A standard deviation of 69 on a mean of 181 means facility workload varies enormously, the busiest facilities see eight times the quietest.
  • These six numbers are what a histogram makes visible. Quote them in the caption and the reader can check the picture against the arithmetic.
  • Nothing here is a summary for the chart: the histogram will use the raw column.

3The wrong chart, for comparison

# a bar chart needs one number per category cases %>% count(district) %>% head(3)
R Consoleoutput

1 Bo          103

Explanation
  • This is what Week 7 needed: a summarised table, one row per bar.
  • A histogram needs none of that. Hand it suspected and it produces its own counts, from bins it works out itself.
  • If you find yourself writing group_by() before a histogram, you are about to plot the wrong thing.

4The shape, in one line

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_histogram()
R Consoleoutput
`stat_bin()` using `bins = 30`. Pick better value with `binwidth`.
Explanation
  • That message is not an error. It is ggplot2 telling you it guessed 30 bins because you did not say, and asking you to choose.
  • Only x is mapped. Mapping y as well is the commonest histogram mistake and gives you a chart of nothing useful.
  • The next card is entirely about answering that message properly.

5What 30 bins actually looks like

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_histogram(bins = 30, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white")
Plots panefigure

the default: 30 bins of about 11 cases each, and a jagged top

Explanation
  • Thirty bins over a range of 330 gives bins about 11 wide, so each holds few facilities and random wobble shows up as spikes.
  • The shape is there: a single hump peaking near 180, but the jaggedness is noise, not data.
  • Compare it with the smooth version on the card banner above: same data, wider bins.

Choosing the bins

The one decision that changes what the reader sees

When to use it

Every histogram you show anybody. The default 30 bins is a guess, and the message ggplot2 prints is asking you to replace it with a number that means something in your units.

How to use it

binwidth in the units of the data: 50 suspected cases: or bins as a count. Prefer binwidth: a reader understands "bars fifty cases wide", not "thirty bars".

What happensillustration

1Bins fifty cases wide

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 50, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white")
Plots panefigure

seven bars, 304 in the tallest, the shape without the noise

Explanation
  • binwidth = 50 gives seven bars, each covering fifty suspected cases. No message is printed, because you have answered the question.
  • The peak bar, 150 to 200, holds 304 facility months. The shape is a single clean hump, slightly longer on the right.
  • This is the version to put in a report: a reader can say "about 300 facilities see between 150 and 200 cases a month" and be right.

2Narrower bins show more, and more noise

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 25, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white")
Plots panefigure

binwidth 25: fifteen bars, and a top that wobbles

Explanation
  • Halving the width doubles the number of bars and halves what each holds, so random variation becomes visible as a rough top.
  • Narrow bins are for exploring: a real second cluster or a gap in the data will survive, while noise will not.
  • Nothing here changed except the width. Any pattern that appears at one width and vanishes at another is not a pattern.

3Too wide, and the shape disappears

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 100, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white")
Plots panefigure

binwidth 100: honest, and useless

Explanation
  • Four bars is not a distribution, it is a summary table drawn badly. The peak, the tail and the symmetry have all gone.
  • The 100 to 200 bar holds 581 of 1,248 facility months, which is true and tells the reader almost nothing.
  • Between 25 and 50 is the useful range for this column. Try three widths and pick the one that shows the shape without the wobble.

4Making the bins start where a reader expects

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 50, boundary = 0, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white")
Plots panefigure

round edges: 0, 50, 100: numbers a reader can repeat

Explanation
  • boundary = 0 forces a bin edge onto zero, so the bars run 0 to 50, 50 to 100 and so on rather than 47 to 97.
  • Without it, ggplot2 centres the bins on the data and your bars start at an arbitrary number nobody can quote.
  • center = 25 does the same job from the middle of a bin. Use one or the other, never both.

5A different column, a different width

ggplot(cases, aes(x = confirmed)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 20, boundary = 0, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white")
Plots panefigure

confirmed cases: a leaning to the right shape with a long tail

Explanation
  • Confirmed cases run from 14 to 196, a narrower range than suspected, so the bin width has to come down with it, 20 here, not 50.
  • The shape is different too: this one leans right, with a long tail of high burden facility months.
  • Bin width is not a setting you find once. It belongs to the column, and it changes when the column does.

When to use it

When the question is whether one group differs from another: Q1 against Q2, one district against the rest, a facility type against another.

How to use it

Map a category to fill, then choose how the bars sit: position = "identity" with alpha overlays them, "stack" piles them, and facet_wrap() gives each group its own panel, usually the clearest of the three.

What happensillustration

1Overlaid, with transparency

cases <- cases %>% mutate(quarter = if_else(month %in% c("January","February","March"), "Q1", "Q2")) ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected, fill = quarter)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 50, position = "identity", alpha = 0.5, colour = "white")
Plots panefigure

the overlap is the point: where Q2 sits to the right of Q1

Explanation
  • position = "identity" draws both groups from the same baseline, so they overlap. Without alpha the second group hides the first.
  • Both quarters have 624 facility months, so the two shapes are directly comparable: Q2 has more in the 250 to 350 bars, which is the seasonal rise.
  • Only use this with two groups. Three overlapping translucent histograms are unreadable.

2Stacked, which is the default and usually wrong

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected, fill = quarter)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 50, colour = "white")
Plots panefigure

the total is right, the comparison is lost

Explanation
  • Leave position out and the groups stack, so each bar is the total of both quarters.
  • The bottom group is readable; the top one is not, because it starts from a different height in every bar. Comparing shapes is impossible.
  • Stacking answers "how many altogether", which a single histogram already answers. Use it only when the total is the message.

3One panel per group

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 60, boundary = 0, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white") + facet_wrap(~ district)
Plots panefigure

four districts, one shared scale

Explanation
  • Faceting is the honest way to compare more than two groups: same axes in every panel, so the shapes and the heights both mean something.
  • The panels have different numbers of facilities. Bo has 103 facility months, Western Urban 48, so the bars are shorter on the right, and that is real.
  • Four panels shown here. With sixteen districts, facet the four that matter and say so, rather than printing a wall of thumbnails.

4Free scales, when the sizes differ too much

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 60, boundary = 0, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white") + facet_wrap(~ district, scales = "free_y")
Plots panefigure

free_y: shape readable, size not comparable

Explanation
  • scales = "free_y" lets each panel use its own height, so a small district's shape is visible instead of squashed.
  • The cost is that the panels can no longer be compared by eye: a tall bar in one panel may be fewer facilities than a short bar in another.
  • Use it to read shape, never to compare size, and label the axes so the reader knows which you meant.

5A rate rather than a count

cases <- cases %>% mutate(tpr = confirmed / tested_rdt * 100) ggplot(cases, aes(x = tpr)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 5, boundary = 20, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white")
Plots panefigure

positivity: wide and flat, so the national average describes nobody

Explanation
  • Test positivity runs from 27 to 66 per cent, so the bin width drops to 5, percentage points, not cases.
  • The shape is almost flat between 30 and 65, which is itself the finding: positivity varies widely and no single national figure describes a facility.
  • A histogram of a rate is often more useful than a histogram of a count, because it is already comparable across facilities of different sizes.

When to use it

When the bars are getting in the way: comparing three or more groups, or showing a shape to an audience that will read bars as exact counts.

How to use it

geom_density() draws a smoothed curve whose area is one, so its y axis is density, not count. To put it over a histogram, the histogram has to change its y axis too: aes(y = after_stat(density)).

What happensillustration

1The curve on its own

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_density(fill = "steelblue", alpha = 0.4, colour = "steelblue")
Plots panefigure

density: shape without bars, and without counts

Explanation
  • geom_density() replaces the bars with a smoothed curve. No bin width to choose, and no jagged top.
  • The y axis is density, not count: the area under the whole curve is 1, so a single number on that axis means nothing on its own.
  • That is the trade. A density curve shows shape beautifully and cannot tell a reader how many facilities are in a range.

2Both together, done properly

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_histogram(aes(y = after_stat(density)), binwidth = 50, fill = "grey80", colour = "white") + geom_density(colour = "#b45309", linewidth = 1.2)
Plots panefigure

the same y axis for both layers, the whole trick

Explanation
  • after_stat(density) tells the histogram to plot density rather than counts, so the two layers share one y axis.
  • Without it the bars are in the hundreds and the curve is in thousandths, and the curve flattens to an invisible line along the bottom. This is the single most common failure with this chart.
  • Grey bars behind a coloured curve is the conventional look: the bars carry the data, the curve carries the eye.

3A frequency polygon, when you want counts and a line

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_freqpoly(binwidth = 50, colour = "steelblue", linewidth = 1.2)
Plots panefigure

counts, as a line: 304 at the peak, same as the bars

Explanation
  • geom_freqpoly() is a histogram drawn as a line: same bins, same counts, one point per bin joined up.
  • It keeps the count axis, which geom_density() gives away, and it takes the same binwidth.
  • This is the honest way to put three or four groups on one chart: three lines are readable, three sets of overlapping bars are not.

4Three groups as curves

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected, colour = quarter)) + geom_density(linewidth = 1.2)
Plots panefigure

one curve per quarter: shapes comparable, sizes hidden

Explanation
  • Mapping colour instead of fill gives one curve per group with no fill to obscure the others.
  • Because each curve integrates to one, groups of different sizes are put on the same footing, useful, and worth saying, because it hides the fact that one group may be far smaller.
  • If group size matters to the message, use faceted histograms instead and let the heights speak.

5Marking the mean and the median

Plots panefigure

mean and median four cases apart: a symmetric distribution

Explanation
  • geom_vline() takes a number, not a column, so the statistic is computed in the call: mean(cases$suspected).
  • Mean 181 and median 177 almost coincide here, which is what a symmetric shape looks like. On the confirmed column they would be further apart.

When to use it

Before you quote a mean, set a threshold, or believe a total. A histogram is a data quality check as much as a chart, and it is the cheapest one you have.

How to use it

Look at four things: which way the tail runs, whether there is more than one hump, whether there is a spike on a round number, and whether anything sits where nothing should. Each one changes what you do next.

What happensillustration

1Skew: which way the tail runs

ggplot(cases, aes(x = confirmed)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 20, boundary = 0, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white") c(mean = mean(cases$confirmed, na.rm = TRUE), median = median(cases$confirmed, na.rm = TRUE))

mean pulled above the median by the tail

Explanation
  • The bars trail off to the right, so the distribution is skewed to the right: a few facility months with very high case loads and none with negative ones.
  • The mean, 71, sits above the median, 66. That gap is the arithmetic signature of a right tail, and it is why the median is the fairer number to quote for a typical facility.

  • This is the shape that justifies targeting: if every facility were near the average there would be nothing to target.

When to use it

The last few minutes before it goes into a bulletin. A histogram needs one thing no other chart does: the bin width in the caption, because the picture changes when the width changes.

How to use it

labs() for the words, scale_x_continuous() for sensible breaks, a theme to clear the furniture, and ggsave() for the file. Say the bin width and the number of observations somewhere on the chart.

What happensillustration

1The words that make it usable

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 50, boundary = 0, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white") + labs(title = "Suspected malaria cases per facility-month", subtitle = "1,248 facility-months, 208 chiefdoms, January to June 2026", x = "Suspected cases per facility-month", y = "Facility-months", caption = "Bars 50 cases wide. Source: routine facility reports.") + theme_minimal()
Plots panefigure

bin width in the caption, real units on both axes

Explanation
  • The y axis is the count of something, and only you know what: say "facility months", not "count".
  • The bin width belongs in the caption. It is the one number a reader needs to reproduce the picture, and the one nobody ever includes.
  • theme_minimal() rather than theme_void() here: a histogram without its axes is unreadable, so keep them.

2Axis breaks a reader can use

ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 50, boundary = 0, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white") + scale_x_continuous(breaks = seq(0, 400, 50)) + scale_y_continuous(expand = expansion(mult = c(0, 0.05))) + theme_minimal()
Plots panefigure

a label on every bin edge

Explanation
  • breaks = seq(0, 400, 50) puts a label on every bin edge, so a reader can name the range a bar covers without counting.
  • The expand line removes the gap under the bars, so they sit on the axis instead of floating above it.
  • Small things, but they are the difference between a chart that looks drafted and one that looks finished.

3When the tail is very long: a log axis

# not needed for this column, but this is how ggplot(cases, aes(x = confirmed)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 0.1, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white") + scale_x_log10() + labs(x = "Confirmed cases (log scale)", y = "Facility-months") + theme_minimal()
Plots panefigure

the technique, on a column that does not really need it

Explanation
  • scale_x_log10() compresses a long right tail so the bulk of the data stops being squashed into two bars.
  • On a log axis the binwidth is in log units, so 0.1 rather than 20. Forgetting that is why the chart usually comes out with one bar.
  • This column does not need it, the tail is mild. Reach for it when the largest value is twenty or more times the median, as with facility catchment populations.

4When a histogram is the wrong chart

# 16 districts: a histogram of district totals has 16 values and tells you nothing district_totals <- cases %>% group_by(district) %>% summarise(confirmed = sum(confirmed, na.rm = TRUE)) nrow(district_totals)
Plots panefigure
[1] 16
Explanation
  • A histogram needs enough values to have a shape. Sixteen district totals is a bar chart. Week 7: not a histogram.
  • The rule of thumb: below about 30 values, draw the values themselves; above it, draw the shape.
  • The other wrong use is a histogram of a category coded as numbers, like chiefdom_code. The bins would be meaningless.

5Saving it

p <- ggplot(cases, aes(x = suspected)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 50, boundary = 0, fill = "steelblue", colour = "white") + labs(title = "Suspected malaria cases per facility-month", x = "Suspected cases", y = "Facility-months", caption = "Bars 50 cases wide, n = 1,248") + theme_minimal() ggsave("suspected_histogram.png", plot = p, width = 8, height = 5, dpi = 300)
Plots panefigure

8 by 5 inches at 300 dpi

Explanation
  • A histogram is wider than it is tall, 8 by 5 inches suits it, unlike the maps in the previous module.
  • 300 dpi for print, 150 for a slide. The file lands in your working directory, which getwd() from Week 1 will tell you.
  • Save the script, not just the picture. The next person to ask for this chart will want it with next month's data.
